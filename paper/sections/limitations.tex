Both units of analysis are approximate. Capture sessions are inferred from
file names, and hash-based clustering merges some distinct photographs. The
held-out-session split covers only the \NHeldOutStrata{} strata that have more
than one session; for the rest we have no session-independent estimate, and the
grouped-split numbers should be read as upper bounds. Where the fresh and stale
images of a produce type came from different devices or apps, freshness and
acquisition source are confounded. We can expose that confound, but removing it
needs new data. The freshness labels are the dataset authors' binary folder
labels, with no inter-rater agreement and no intermediate ripeness stages. Most
images show a single item on a plain background. Section~\ref{sec:phone}
measures what this costs on web and COCO photos, but we did not collect field
images in markets. CIFAR-10 images are small and upsampled, which makes them an
easy far-OOD test. Our latency figures, including those of the two-stage scan,
come from a laptop CPU and an Android emulator rather than a range of real
phones. Finally, the quality grade and shelf life shown to users are
unvalidated heuristics.

The phone-photo tests are small or only partly labelled. The web set has
\WebN{} photos plus two of our own, and we chose the v2.0.1 gate while looking
at them, so its web results are optimistic. The v2.1 gates were set on
validation data, but the false-accept rate they match is the one v2.0.1 reached
at that gate. The detector's COCO AP is a lower bound, because produce that
COCO does not annotate, such as tomatoes and peppers, counts against it. At
\DetInput{} pixels SSDlite misses small items in crowded scenes and finds only
\ScanMedFound\% of the medium COCO fruit. False accepts on unsupported produce
were matched at \CalWhole\% on validation but reach \ScanOodDet\% on the test
half.
