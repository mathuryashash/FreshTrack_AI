Papers on produce freshness classification regularly report accuracy close to
100\% on public Kaggle datasets. We took a close look at one of the most popular
of these datasets and found that part of that accuracy reflects how the data are
split and, more importantly, how the photographs were taken. Its \NImages{}
images collapse into \NGroups{} near-duplicate clusters, drawn from roughly
\NSessions{} capture sessions. A random per-file split puts a copy of
\NaiveLeakPct\% of the test images into training, and an EfficientNet-B0 then
scores \NaiveFreshAcc\% on freshness. Keeping each cluster on one side of the
split gives \MtlFreshAcc\%, yet \SessionSharePct\% of those test images still
share a capture session with training images. When whole sessions are held out,
freshness accuracy falls to \SessFreshAcc\% and calibration error rises from
\MtlFreshEce\% to \SessFreshEce\%. The failures cluster where fresh and stale
photographs of the same produce came from different cameras or messaging apps:
on an unseen session of stale capsicum the model is right on only
\SessHeldCapsicumStale\% of images. On the photo-grouped split, joint training
of freshness and produce type performs on par with separate models, and a
multi-task MobileNetV3-Large reaches \MnvFreshAcc\% freshness accuracy in
\MnvCpuMs\,ms on a laptop CPU. An energy-score gate on its produce-type head
rejects \MnvGateFarOODRejectRate\% of non-produce images but only
\MnvGateNearOODRejectRate\% of unsupported produce. We release the splits, code
and an Android app that runs the model offline, and we argue that shelf life
and quality grade, for which these datasets hold no ground truth, should not be
presented as learned outputs.
